Long ago we had surrendered nearly all our outdoor public spaces entirely to parking cars. Parking lots sprawled and oozed between buildings, pushing suburbs and cities further apart and adding hours to traffic time. We destroyed our cities infrastructure in search of free parking, violent disputes over parking spaces were normalized and trivialized. 

Developers chopped the stoops off townhouses and apartments to create more lanes for traffic, trading porches for parking. We cared more about housing for our cars than housing for ourselves. A maze of driveways, garaged doors and concrete parking pads tore the social and environmental fabric, we destroyed third spaces so that only cars were allowed to exist outside. 

 Empty, sprawling parking lots turned neighborhoods into heat islands, and flooding resulted in catastrophic losses. We had lost our way. 

So we change it. We started by banning parking minimums, and returned strips of dedicated parking spaces back into bus lanes, saplings and restaurant patios. We depaved, returning asphalt to prairies and wetlands woven into our cities and towns, which stopped the floods and fires. 

We rolled back urban sprawl, and without parked cars dominating the streets, our neighborhoods became walkable again. We invested in public transportation and bike lanes, our sidewalks were expanded and maintained so that wheelchairs and strollers and walkers had shaded dignity again. 

The roads were returned to the people, children and teens could bike to places on their own again, and a culture of micromobility replaced car culture. Third places moved into the home garages, parking garages, parking lots and car parks. They became colorful touchstones of our community instead of exhaust-filled buildings. No longer did we fight over parking spaces, instead we met each other on the streets as neighbors and friends. 

Parking spaces became our missing third places.
